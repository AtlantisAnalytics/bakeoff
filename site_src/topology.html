Multi-GPU topology

Can two or three cheap 12 GB cards stand in for one big one? In November 2026 we ran four models on one machine with three RTX 3060s, split several ways, and measured speed and usable context. This is an exploration, not a tier: these runs are not part of any week, and they never change a pick or a verdict. Every number below is read from the raw bundles, which are published like every other result.

Short version

  • Two cards with tensor parallelism pay off. Decode is 1.7× one card's, and models and contexts that don't fit on one 12 GB card do fit on two.
  • Against a 24 GB RTX 3090 Ti, two 3060s decode at 60–72% of its speed for 62–67% of its power.
  • A third card adds little. Tensor parallelism over three cards was 8% faster than over two, for 35% more power.
  • How the model is split matters more than how many cards there are. Splitting by layers was 1.7× slower than tensor parallelism on the same two cards, and a pipeline over three cards was no faster than one card.
  • Crossing CPU sockets cost almost nothing on this machine: 0.5% of decode speed.

The machine

A dual-socket workstation with two Intel Xeon Gold 5215 CPUs and 125 GiB of RAM, running Ubuntu 24.04 and NVIDIA driver 570. It has three RTX 3060 12 GB cards at their stock 170 W limit. Each card has its own PCIe 3.0 x16 link to a CPU: card 0 to the first socket, cards 1 and 2 to the second. GeForce cards don't support peer-to-peer transfers, so every byte between cards goes through the CPUs. Each bundle records every card's PCIe link and NUMA node and the path between each pair of cards, as nvidia-smi topo reports it.

How to read the tables

  • Split: tensor parallel divides every layer across the cards, which work on each token together. A layer split or a pipeline gives each card a run of whole layers, so for a single request one card works while the others wait.
  • Cards used: the cards that held at least 1 GiB during the run. A split can leave a card idle.
  • Farthest link: the longest path between any two of the run's cards.
  • Decode is the median tokens/s of one request, short prompt. 4 streams is the total tokens/s of four requests at once. TTFT is the time to the first token. Context is the largest context that passed the fit test. Power is the mean board power of all the run's cards during the speed test (CPU power not included).
  • Rows marked with a week are published weekly runs, on the same engines.

Granite 4.2 3B (vLLM)

The small model shows the scaling most clearly. Our own single-card run decodes within 2% of the published weekly one. Two cards in tensor parallel decode 1.71× faster, and serve four streams 1.77× faster. Putting the two cards on different sockets made no real difference. A three-card pipeline was exactly as fast as one card, at 65% more power.

Granite 4.2 8B FP8 (vLLM)

On one 12 GB card the FP8 weights leave too little memory for the KV cache. We could only serve 4K of context, well short of the 16K minimum our rules require. Two cards reach 32K, and decode 1.78× faster than one, at 72% of a 3090 Ti's decode speed and 67% of its power.

Qwen3.8 27B EXL3 (ExLlamaV3)

A 27B model at 4 bits doesn't fit on one 3060, so every row here is split. Tensor parallelism over two cards decodes 1.66× faster than a layer split over the same two. The "three-card" layer split put the whole model on the first two cards and left the third empty. ExLlamaV3's automatic split fills cards in order. Tensor parallelism over three cards gained 8% in decode and 11% in four-stream throughput over two, for 35% more power. Two cards reach 69% of the 3090 Ti's decode speed.

Gemma 4 12B IT EXL3 (ExLlamaV3)

Two cards in tensor parallel hold the full 128K context and decode at 60% of the 3090 Ti's speed. Long prompts are where the 3060s trail most: prefill on a long prompt ran at 4,694 tokens/s, against 9,021 on the 3090 Ti, about half.

Setup notes

  • vLLM on GeForce cards. Without peer-to-peer support, vLLM falls back from its own all-reduce to NCCL. Tensor parallel start-up then ran out of memory while capturing CUDA graphs, at both 0.92 and 0.88 of card memory, because graph memory sits outside that budget. Setting VLLM_MEMORY_PROFILER_ESTIMATE_CUDAGRAPHS=1 makes vLLM count graph memory before it sizes the KV cache; with it, every multi-GPU vLLM run started at 0.90. Single-card runs keep the weekly 0.92.
  • ExLlamaV3 tensor parallel uses the CPU. During these runs the server process used up to about 15 CPU cores, so a machine with fewer cores may be slower. The bundles don't record this; we saw it in top.
  • Check which cards a split used. Look at each card's memory, as the Cards used column does.

Limits

  • One machine, one run of five repeats per setup. Another board, another PCIe generation or NVLink would change the link results. The socket result was measured only on the 3B model.
  • Speed and fit only: no quality suites were run. A split changes the order of floating-point sums, so outputs can differ slightly from a single card's, as they do between cards.
  • The multi-GPU vLLM runs used 0.90 of card memory and the single-card runs 0.92, as noted above.
  • The 3090 Ti rows are from a different machine and week, and serve as a reference point, not a controlled comparison.

Reproduce

The bundles, and a list of which run is in which table, are in results/experiments/2026-11-multi-gpu/ in the repository. Each bundle names the harness commit that made it, and the engine versions: vLLM 0.19.0, and ExLlamaV3 1.6.3 served by TabbyAPI. To run a setup yourself, pick the cards, serve, and run the speed and fit passes:

CUDA_VISIBLE_DEVICES=1,2 VLLM_MEMORY_PROFILER_ESTIMATE_CUDAGRAPHS=1 \
  vllm serve <weights> --tensor-parallel-size 2 \
  --gpu-memory-utilization 0.90 --max-model-len 32768
uv run bakeoff run run.toml --only speed --only fit

with envelope = "2× RTX 3060 12 GB" and parallel = "tp=2" in the run file's [model] table. For ExLlamaV3, set tensor_parallel: true (tensor parallel) or gpu_split_auto: true (layer split) in TabbyAPI's model config.

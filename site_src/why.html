Why bakeoff

How this started

This started in 2019, while I was finishing grad school. Back then, the cost of entry into AI was a laptop with an i7, 64 GB of RAM, an RTX 2080 Super, a couple of terabytes of storage, and an eye for curating datasets.

When ChatGPT arrived in late 2022, that world blinked out of existence for small players like me. Without nation-state budgets, we weren't going to play in that league any more. Imagine McLaren, Mercedes, Ferrari and Red Bull showing up at your local autocross and never leaving town. It changes the game.

Then there's the price. We've watched ride-hailing, delivery and short-term rentals get cheap enough to win their markets, then get expensive. If AI tools go the same way, I want to keep at least some of what we had from 2023 to 2025, on hardware I own.

I'm an engineer and a data scientist, not a software person. These tools let me solve problems and express ideas I couldn't before. Losing them would be like giving a dog the ability to speak and then slowly taking it away. That would be beyond cruel, and the dog would probably try to find a way to keep it.

Enter open-weight models, quantization, agents for everything, and hope. While exploring OpenClaw and Hermes in early 2026, I realized that if this gnarly, uncharted space could be wrangled, the barrier to entry could come back down far enough for individuals to compete again.

I wanted a way to keep up with the deluge of new models and pick the best one for each machine in my homelab. I started testing models by hand, just to see which ones would run. I soon realized this was a job for an agent on the night shift, and one that should pit models not just against my hardware but against each other. A competition of sorts. A bakeoff, if you will.

What it does

Every weekend, bakeoff runs a selection of new open-weight models on cards people actually own: a 12 GB RTX 3060, a 24 GB RTX 3090 Ti and a 36 GB Apple M4 Max. The engines are pinned, every model gets the same tests, and each run's raw results are published so anyone can check the numbers. On Monday, it says plainly which model to run on each card, and whether it's worth switching.

Who it's for

If you have a machine you want to keep using, and want the best model for it this week without reading every release thread, this is for you. How we test is on the method page, and who pays for it is on the independence page.
